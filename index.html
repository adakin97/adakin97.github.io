Running Earth observation · OSINT

Damage Maps

Assessing structural damage in conflict zones is slow and dangerous on the ground, and cloud cover defeats optical satellites. Radar sees through both. This pipeline runs on Google Earth Engine: a NASA FIRMS thermal feed flags candidate sites within hours of an event, then the PWTT change-detection algorithm, developed at UCL, tests Sentinel-1 radar time series for structural change. I built the ingestion, processing, validation and mapping around the algorithm.

Applied across seven areas, including Bakhmut, Tehran, Natanz and Al Udeid. At one site it surfaced structural change that had not been reported in open sources at the time of analysis.

Ask me about: radar backscatter versus optical, why validation is the hard part, and where a person still beats the algorithm.

Interactive Urban growth · Remote sensing

Shenzhen, watched from orbit

In the late 1970s Shenzhen was a market town across the border from Hong Kong. Today it is a city of around seventeen million. I used Landsat archives and three decades of nighttime-lights data to measure that growth from space. Drag the slider: the upper half of the frame is Shenzhen, the lower half is Hong Kong.

Displacement pressure model

When conflict displaces people, ground reporting lags days or weeks behind the movement itself. I am building a model that reads WorldPop population rasters, VIIRS nighttime lights and MODIS vegetation indices as proxy signals for population movement under pressure. The hard problem is honesty: lights dim for many reasons, and the model has to say what it cannot see.

Peatland recovery, tested properly

Did the UK's peatland restoration schemes actually work? Sentinel-2 vegetation and moisture series over restored sites and matched controls, deseasonalised, then a difference-in-differences design around each site's restoration date. This is the economics training doing real work on satellite data: a causal answer, not a before and after picture.

How I think about data

The projects differ. The discipline behind them does not.

Rules before models

At Bloomberg I used a scikit-learn classifier to analyse a framework of 5,000+ business rules and work out which should survive a data-model migration. The lesson stuck. Most data quality problems are governance problems, and a model only helps once you understand the rules it sits on.

Humans in the loop, on purpose

A good pipeline decides what a machine should do and where a person must check. The ingestion workflow I built at Bloomberg routed exceptions to analysts rather than passing them silently. My damage pipeline flags candidate sites automatically but nothing gets called damage until a person has looked. Knowing what not to automate is the skill.

Validation is the deliverable

A detection nobody can trust is noise. The interesting work is the checking: baselines, false positives, and what the sensor cannot see. This page says "in build" wherever something is unfinished, because a claim without evidence behind it costs more than it earns.

Write it down

Data only changes decisions when someone who does not write code can act on it. At Bloomberg I was the main point of contact for the EMEA ESG loans dataset and turned it into written market research, including two editions of LMA Horizons read by banks and asset managers.

About
