Approach

How I think about data

The projects differ. The discipline behind them does not.

Rules before models

At Bloomberg I used a scikit-learn classifier to analyse a framework of 5,000+ business rules and work out which should survive a data-model migration. The lesson stuck. Most data quality problems are governance problems, and a model only helps once you understand the rules it sits on.

Humans in the loop, on purpose

A good pipeline decides what a machine should do and where a person must check. The ingestion workflow I built at Bloomberg routed exceptions to analysts rather than passing them silently. In my radar work, nothing gets called damage until a person has looked. Knowing what not to automate is the skill.

Validation is the deliverable

A detection nobody can trust is noise. The interesting work is the checking: baselines, controls, false positives, and what the sensor cannot see. This site says "in build" wherever something is unfinished, because a claim without evidence behind it costs more than it earns.

Write it down

Data only changes decisions when someone who does not write code can act on it. At Bloomberg I was the main point of contact for the EMEA ESG loans dataset and turned it into written market research, including two editions of LMA Horizons read by banks and asset managers.

Earth observation · self-directed · 2026

Damage Maps

Assessing structural damage in conflict zones is slow and dangerous on the ground, and cloud and smoke defeat optical satellites. Radar sees through both. I applied a published change-detection method, developed at UCL, to Sentinel-1 radar time series across seven areas, and built the pipeline around it.

Status
Built 2026
My part
Data pipeline, the runs, mapping and checks. I did not write the algorithm.
Data
Sentinel-1 SAR, NASA FIRMS thermal anomalies
Tools
Google Earth Engine, Python

The question

Can open radar data show where structures changed after an event, quickly, and without anyone on the ground? Sentinel-1 passes over most places every few days and its radar returns do not care about cloud or night.

How the method works

The pixel-wise t-test compares each pixel's radar backscatter in a window before a date with a window after it. A large, consistent shift gets flagged. The method's own code also masks out non-urban land and smooths across several scales so that a single noisy pixel does not trigger a detection. Those steps are part of the published method, not additions of mine.

What I built

For each area: pulling the Sentinel-1 series on Earth Engine, running the method around an event date, mapping the output, and sense-checking it against published building footprints. Separately, in March 2026, I built a small monitor that polled NASA FIRMS thermal anomalies at six sites and, five days after an alert, emailed me a reminder to run the radar check by hand once post-event imagery existed. It logged from 20 to 31 March, recorded no anomalies, and is not running now. The public version of that code queues the radar check automatically when the analysis libraries are available.

The control

North London is on the list on purpose. I pointed the identical pipeline at a city where nothing had happened on the test date, to see what a false-positive floor looks like before trusting any detection elsewhere.

What it cannot do

It detects change, not damage. New construction, flooding and large parked vehicles all change radar backscatter. That is why a person looks before anything is called damage, and why I would never quote a detection rate for this work: the method's published validation is the author's, not mine.

Earth observation · urban growth · 2026

Shenzhen, watched from orbit

In the late 1970s Shenzhen was a market town across the border from Hong Kong. Today it is a city of around seventeen million. I used Landsat archives and three decades of nighttime-lights data to measure that growth from space.

Status
Interactive
Data
Landsat surface reflectance, DMSP-OLS and VIIRS nighttime lights
Tools
Google Earth Engine
Write-up
Essay in draft

The question

How fast did Shenzhen actually grow, and where? Population statistics for fast-growing Chinese cities undercount migrants, so a physical measure from space is a useful second opinion.

What the sensors can and cannot show

The two lights sensors differ, and DMSP saturates over bright city cores, so the night images are maps of extent rather than comparable brightness. Landsat shows the land itself turning from farmland and fish ponds to built surface. Being straight about what a sensor cannot show is half the job.

Earth observation · humanitarian data · in build

Displacement pressure model

When conflict displaces people, ground reporting lags days or weeks behind the movement itself. I am building a model that reads open satellite and population data as proxy signals for movement under pressure. It is unfinished and unvalidated, and this page says so.

Status
In build
Data
WorldPop population, VIIRS nighttime lights, MODIS vegetation index
What it gave me
A working knowledge of open geospatial data I now use across projects

The question

Can open data flag where population pressure is building before anyone reports it? The honest problem is that every proxy moves for several reasons. Lights dim when people leave, but also when the grid fails. Vegetation changes when fields are abandoned, but also with the season.

The approach

Combine signals so that each rules out the others' false alarms, and make the model say what it cannot see. A town whose lights dim while the vegetation stays normal and a nearby settlement brightens tells a different story from a town where everything dims at once.

Next

Pick one past displacement with good ground records and test whether the combined signal would have flagged it, before building anything general.

Earth observation · causal inference · in build

Peatland recovery, tested properly

Did the UK's peatland restoration schemes actually work? Almost every satellite project is a classification or a change map. This one treats restoration as a quasi-experiment and asks for a causal answer.

Status
In build
Data
Sentinel-2 moisture and vegetation indices; restored sites and dates from the UK Land Carbon Registry
Method
Difference-in-differences with matched controls, event study
Tools
Google Earth Engine, Python

The question

Did restored peatland diverge from comparable unrestored sites after the intervention, and can the intervention be detected from space without being told when it happened?

The design

Monthly cloud-masked Sentinel-2 composites of moisture (NDMI) and vegetation (NDVI) for each restored site and a matched unrestored control, with the number of clear pixels carried alongside because cloud loss is not random. The difference-in-differences estimate compares the change at restored sites with the change at controls over the same months. The validation is blind: breakpoint detection on the index series, compared afterwards with restoration dates the method never saw.

What could break it

The whole design rests on parallel trends: without restoration, both groups would have moved together. Move the third slider above and watch the estimate go wrong while the chart still looks convincing. Spillovers to nearby controls and cloud cover that differs by season are the other two threats I am watching.

Next

The analysis code runs on simulated data. Next is a real site table from the registry, and one site's raw moisture series plotted before any modelling.

Retrieval-augmented AI · day job · prototype

A library assistant that answers only from its sources

An international NGO network I work for holds around twenty years of security guidance: about 1,250 guides, toolkits and papers. People struggle to find the right one unless they already speak the vocabulary. I am building an assistant that takes a plain question, searches the library and answers only from what it finds, with a link to every source.

Status
Prototype
Built
Catalogue, test set, working prototype
Tools
Python, BM25 search, Claude API
Note
Prototype built with Claude Code; public content only

Why retrieval, not training

Training a model on the library would be expensive, could not cite its sources, and would go stale the day a new guide appeared. Retrieval searches the library at question time, so new guidance counts at once and every answer can be checked against its source.

What I built

First, a catalogue of the whole public library, collected politely from the site's sitemaps at one request a second: type, date, language, authorship and files for every item, with automatic flags for guidance that may be out of date. Then 53 test questions across finding a resource, explaining a term, other languages, sensitive questions, emergencies and out-of-scope requests, each matched to the resources a good answer should cite. Then the prototype.

Safety rules

An emergency gets a fixed message, never a generated one. The assistant points to guidance rather than advising on a live situation. It never invents a resource; if the library does not cover a question, it says so.

Next

Real member questions to replace my drafts, then the same questions through three build routes (our own build, a website plugin, a hosted product), judged on answer quality first and on whether someone other than me could run it.

Risk modelling · day job · testing

Testing a model of what security spending returns

A colleague built a prototype that estimates the return on an NGO's security spending. The model is theirs. My part is to test it: run its maths in code, see which inputs move the answer, and write up what should change.

Status
Testing
My part
Review, testing and a list of eleven fixes
Method
Expected loss, Bayesian updating, Monte Carlo

The problem it tackles

Security budgets are hard to defend because the return is losses that never happened, and nobody ever observes it. The model works out an expected loss with and without the measures, then the return on what the measures cost, with ranges from 2,000 simulated years.

What I found

The headline should be the simulation's middle run rather than one central calculation, because a long right tail pulls the average up: move the sliders above and watch the mean and median separate. Overlapping measures can double-count, several inputs carry no range at all so the uncertainty shown is too narrow, and a bad year should not raise the return.

The closest comparison

Insurance. Security costs a little more than it saves in a typical year and saves a great deal in a bad one. That framing persuades better than a single return figure.

Next

Agree the fixes with the author, then calibrate reference rates against open incident data. A beta with the fixes was built with Claude Code for the author to take or leave.

Data governance · day job · in progress

Mapping an organisation's data, then cleaning it up

Before any AI tool touches an organisation's data, someone has to know where that data lives and how it moves. I am mapping the systems behind a membership network and fixing how contacts and organisations are categorised, so that its impact figures can actually be counted.

Status
In progress
Building
Data map, improvements register, new categories
Rule
Structure, never records

What I am building

A data map that doubles as the organisation's UK GDPR record of processing. An improvements register: every possible fix with a stable ID, the evidence behind it and its status, so nothing is changed on a hunch. And a new way of categorising contacts and organisations, agreed with the team and migrated in bulk.

How I am doing it

I work from exports of the CRM's objects, fields and links, never from member records. Every change goes through sign-off, is tried on a handful of records first, and has a backup export as its undo.

Why it comes first

Categories drift, duplicate fields multiply and reports stop agreeing with each other. Any automation built on top inherits the mess. Rules before models.

Data engineering · energy · in build

GB grid constraint costs

Great Britain pays wind farms to switch off when the network cannot carry their power to where it is needed. The assets that could soak up that power are spread in a pattern nobody chose. I am building a warehouse to put numbers on the mismatch.

Status
In build
Stack
DuckDB locally, dbt models and tests, BigQuery as the target
Why this shape
Chosen to force real warehouse work, not a folder of notebooks

The question

Where is the mismatch between where flexibility physically exists, such as heat pumps, batteries and electric vehicles, and where the grid procures it or pays to curtail generation?

Why the data forces good habits

The energy performance certificate register runs to about 28 million rows, which forces a warehouse. Half-hourly settlement data forces window functions. Postcode to small area to grid supply point forces real joins. Reproducibility forces dbt. Starting in DuckDB means the move to BigQuery is a change of configuration rather than of concept.

Status

The first models run on simulated data shaped like the real thing, with defects planted on purpose: inconsistent postcodes, duplicate certificates and missing lookups. The tests catch them. Next is one real local authority's certificates, then the settlement and constraint data.

Public data · data cleaning · live

Petrol price tracker

A mobile-first interactive built from the government's Fuel Finder data, showing how far unleaded prices rose across 2026, forecourt by forecourt.

Status
Live
Data
347,612 price reports from 8,148 forecourts
Tools
Python, DuckDB, D3

The cleaning is the project

Forecourts only report when their price changes, so a plain average over raw rows over-weights the busiest reporters. Each forecourt's last price of the day is carried forward until its next report, for at most 30 days, and averages are taken across forecourts. Prices outside a band around each day's national median are dropped, which catches typing errors such as prices entered in pounds. Brand spellings are merged, and every forecourt's coordinates are checked against its own postcode.

Checks

Against the RAC's published averages, the pipeline gives 132.94p against 132.83p on 1 March and 153.66p against 152.83p on 31 March. On the 4,688 forecourts with a price on both 1 February and 1 October, the rise is 43.0p; the headline uses a rounder 42p.

Learning · personal · in use

Peripatos: a learning grove

I have learnt a lot of maths, statistics and economics and forgotten more of it. Peripatos turns what I come across each day, at work or in reading, into short study cards, and grows each one as a tree as I recall it. The name is the covered walk at Aristotle's Lyceum, where teaching happened on foot.

Status
In use
Topics
52, across maths, data science, economics and computing
Built
With Claude Code, to my brief. Private for now

How it works

A path shows everything I have planted in the order I met it, with forks suggesting what to learn next, starting with gaps underneath what I already know. Daily walks are spaced repetition: a plant I recall comes back after 1, 2, 5, 12, 30 and then 75 days, and one I neglect wilts. Claude turns a note such as "gamma distribution, seen in a risk model" into a card and names the topics around it.

The detail I like

Each tree is drawn by a recursive function seeded by its topic, so the same topic always grows the same way, and recursion is itself one of the topics in the grove.

Next

Personal data · music · live

Side A / Side B

Thirteen years of my Spotify listening, from November 2013 to October 2026, cleaned, enriched with open music data and turned into a dashboard friends can explore.

Status
Live
Data
Spotify extended streaming history, MusicBrainz, Deezer, ReccoBeats
Tools
Python, ECharts

The pipeline

One clean table from the raw export, with IP addresses dropped. Genres from MusicBrainz for the 6,000 most-played artists, which cover 95% of listening time, at the one request a second MusicBrainz allows. Deezer album genres fill in many of the artists MusicBrainz could not tag, most-played first, which took coverage past 95% before the long tail of small artists stopped being worth the time. Valence, energy and tempo for every track from ReccoBeats. Every lookup is cached and resumable.

What it taught me

Genre is the weakest part of music data. Two open sources disagree on what a genre is, coverage falls off a cliff in the long tail, and one artist rarely fits one label. That is where the next project starts.
